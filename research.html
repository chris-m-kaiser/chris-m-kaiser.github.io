Research

I study the industrial organization of labor markets, focusing on unions, monopsony power, and the effects of public policy. My methodological experience includes structural modeling and demand estimation, alongside causal inference methods such as instrumental variables, staggered-treatment difference-in-differences (DiD), and synthetic DiD. I have also developed experience with and an interest in design-based statistical inference when standard asymptotic approximations are unreliable.

Publications

Minimum Wage Laws and Job Search

Journal of Public Economics · 2026

With David Neumark ↗, Vitor Melo ↗, Liya Palagashvili ↗, and Michael D. Farren ↗

Abstract. Simple theoretical models of job search can imply that a higher minimum wage increases the number of job seekers for affected jobs. Researchers often appeal to these models in defending or explaining non-negative estimated employment effects of minimum wages. We use novel data on job search in U.S. states to examine the effect of minimum wage increases on the number of job seekers for low-skilled positions. We find little if any evidence that higher minimum wages increase job search for low-skilled jobs, and more evidence that higher minimum wages decrease the number of workers seeking employment in these jobs.

Assessing the Impact of Worker Reclassification: Employment Outcomes Post–California AB5

Mercatus Center Working Paper · 2024

With Liya Palagashvili ↗, Paola A. Suarez ↗, and Vitor Melo ↗

Abstract. Using Current Population Survey data and a difference-in-differences design, we assess the employment effects of California’s AB5 worker reclassification law by comparing nonexempt occupations in California with those in states whose classification rules remained unchanged. We estimate average declines of 10.5 percent in self-employment and 4.4 percent in overall employment, with no robust evidence of increased traditional employment. Declines were larger in occupations with a higher prevalence of self-employed workers.

Markets for Worker Representation:
Evidence from Chile

Work in progress

With Sebastián Gallardo ↗

Abstract. We study how competition among unions affects wages when multiple unions may represent workers within the same firm. Motivated by Chile's institutional setting, we develop a model of differentiated worker preferences, bilateral union-firm bargaining, and union entry financed by membership dues. Additional unions strengthen incentives to negotiate higher wages to attract future members, but fragment bargaining leverage by dividing workers across smaller organizations. The model characterizes these opposing forces and compares free entry with a policy allowing at most one union, as well as with the financially feasible number of unions that maximizes worker welfare. Our empirical work draws on Chilean firm surveys and union administrative records to describe representation markets and assess how these mechanisms can be taken to the data.
